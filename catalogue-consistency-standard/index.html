A standard, with the method attached

The catalogue consistency standard.

A single good image proves very little. What a catalogue buyer is actually paying for is the twenty-fourth frame matching the first — same white, same crop logic, same edge, whatever the garment happens to be. That is measurable, so here are the four measurements, the numbers from a real set, and the method written so you can run it on whoever retouches your images now.

What can actually be measured

Four numbers, not an opinion.

Most arguments about retouching quality are arguments about taste, and they cannot be settled. These four can be, by anybody, on a batch they have already paid for.

Run it yourself

The method, including on us.

This is deliberately written so you can point it at your current supplier rather than at us. If it turns out their set holds, you have learned something worth more than a sales call. If it does not, you now have the numbers rather than a feeling.

1. Frame dimension

Read the dimensions of every finished frame in one batch. Any tool that reports pixel size will do — Adobe Bridge, a file inspector, or one command:

magick identify -format "%f %wx%h\n" *.jpg

Sort the output. A set held to a specification returns one line repeated. Count the distinct values; that count is your first number.

2. Background value

Sample the same corner patch on every frame and read its mean brightness. In Photoshop it is the eyedropper with a 5 by 5 average; as a command it is a corner crop and a mean:

magick img.jpg -gravity NorthWest -crop 24x24+8+8 +repage \ -colorspace Gray -format "%[fx:round(255*mean)]\n" info:

On pure white, every frame should return 255. Anything between 248 and 254 is the number that produces a grey rectangle behind the product on a white category page. Check a corner the product never reaches, and check the same corner every time.

3. Crop and margin logic

Open twenty frames as a contact sheet or a grid. You are not looking at the products; you are looking at whether they sit at the same size on the same baseline. Dimensions can be identical while the margins are not, and the grid is where that shows.

4. Edge treatment at 100 per cent

Find the hardest frame in the batch — lace, sheer fabric, hair, wire, fur, anything translucent — and zoom to full resolution. Easy frames tell you nothing, because every supplier gets a solid cotton t-shirt right. The difficult frame is the whole test, and it is invisible at thumbnail size, which is why it survives a review and fails on a product page.

Then ask the supplier one question: is there a written specification for this account, and may I see it? A set that measures consistently without a written specification is consistent by luck and one staffing change away from drifting.

Being honest about it

What these numbers do not tell you.

  • Measurement catches drift, not taste. A garment can be cut to exactly the right dimensions on exactly the right background and still have its collar rebuilt in the wrong shape. The four checks tell you whether a supplier works to a standard. They do not tell you whether the standard is any good.
  • One perfect batch is not a pattern. First batches get attention everywhere. Measure the third and the tenth; that is where a supplier's real process shows.
  • A consistent set can still be the wrong set. If the specification says the wrong thing, it will be wrong identically on every frame — which is harder to spot, not easier.
  • None of it reads turnaround. Consistency and speed are separate promises and they fail separately.
Why sets drift

Four causes, all of them process.

  • No written specification, so every retoucher decides. The specification is the document that makes consistency repeatable; without it, consistency depends on memory.
  • Several retouchers on one account with no shared reference frame. Each is doing good work against a slightly different idea of correct.
  • No agreed colour truth. When two source frames of the same product disagree, somebody has to have decided in advance which frame is the truth. If nobody has, the answer changes by the hour.
  • Peak volume moved to unchecked hands. This is the common one, and it is why we say in writing that when volume moves to a contracted partner studio, every image still passes our own in-house QC before it is delivered and the client is told before their work moves.

All four are process failures rather than skill failures, and all four appear in the measurements before they appear to a buyer. That is the entire argument for measuring.

FAQ

Consistency — questions

What is catalogue consistency?

The property of a finished set in which every frame shares the same measurable treatment: the same pixel dimensions, the same background value, the same crop and margin logic, and the same edge quality, whatever the product happens to be. It is a measurable property rather than a matter of taste, which is why it can be audited.

How do I check whether my retoucher is consistent?

Take a finished batch you have already paid for and measure four things across every frame: pixel dimensions, background brightness sampled in the same corner, the proportion of the frame the product occupies, and edge quality at full resolution on the hardest material in the set. One dimension and one background value across the whole batch means the supplier is working to a specification. Several of either means they are working image by image.

Why does a background value of 253 instead of 255 matter?

It does not matter on one image and it matters a great deal across a grid. A category page puts twenty or forty frames side by side on a white page, and the eye reads the difference as a grey rectangle around the product. Each image passes inspection on its own. The page does not.

What measurements do you publish?

A set of twenty-four finished garments covering seven different material problems. Measured across it: every frame 2600 by 3400, background brightness 255 on all of them, no variance.

Can a set be perfectly consistent and still be badly retouched?

Yes, and it is worth saying so. Measurement catches drift, not taste. Judge the standard itself by looking at the hardest frame in the set at full resolution.
