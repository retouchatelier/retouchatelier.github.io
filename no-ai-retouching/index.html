Why buyers ask

Four contractual reasons, not an aesthetic preference.

  • Licensed imagery. A licence that restricts derivative works, or is silent on AI processing, makes generative editing a risk your legal team has to price.
  • Model and talent releases. A release usually covers photography of a person. It rarely covers synthesis of one, or alteration of their likeness by a model.
  • Design-sensitive products. In jewellery, watches and engineered parts, an invented prong, link or seam is not a retouching choice — it is a misrepresentation of the item being sold.
  • Stock and editorial pipelines with their own rules. Getty Images' contributor modification requirements, for instance, allow extensive retouching but specifically exclude the use of generative AI tools. If any of your imagery flows into a pipeline like that, your retoucher's tooling is your compliance problem.
The distinction that matters

Machine learning is not generative AI.

A supplier who blurs these two is worth a second question, and a supplier who claims to use no machine learning at all is either confused or not telling you the truth.

Verify it

Four checks, and you should run them on us too.

  • Put it in the contract. An assurance in an email is not a term. If a supplier will say it, they should sign it.
  • Ask whether it covers overflow and partner studios. This is where most no-AI promises quietly end.
  • Ask for layered working files on a sample job. Hand retouching leaves a layer history and a working path. A generated frame does not.
  • Ask who the sub-processors are. A supplier who cannot name them cannot promise what happens to your files. Ours are named in the DPA.

We would rather you ran these than took our word for it — and if a competitor passes all four, that is a good supplier and you should say yes to them.

FAQ

Generative AI and your files — questions

Which retouching companies will sign a DPA and not use my images for AI training?

Ask any supplier for three things in writing: that your files are never processed through generative AI tools, that they are never used as training data for any model, and that they will sign your data processing agreement as processor, with Standard Contractual Clauses where the transfer requires them. We commit to all three, and to the same terms at any partner studio used for overflow.

Why would a brand require that its product images are not touched by generative AI?

Four contractual reasons: licensed imagery with restrictions on derivative works, model and talent releases that cover photography but not synthesis, design-sensitive products where an invented detail misrepresents the item, and stock or editorial pipelines with their own rules — Getty's contributor requirements, for example, exclude generative AI tools.

Is using Photoshop's AI selection tools the same as generative AI?

No. Subject selection and edge detection analyse pixels that are already in your file. Generative fill and text-to-image create pixels that were never photographed. Only the second produces content that was not in the licensed original, and only the second is what we rule out.

How can I verify the commitment rather than believe it?

Put it in the contract, ask whether it extends to overflow and partner studios, ask for layered working files on a sample job, and ask who the sub-processors are. Run all four on us.

Does Retouch Atelier use AI anywhere?

Not on client images. We do offer hand correction of images the client has generated themselves, which is the opposite arrangement and is kept separate.

Where is the work done, and does that affect the DPA?

Bangladesh, stated openly. Bangladesh is not on the UK or EU adequacy list, so transfers need Standard Contractual Clauses and, for UK data, the International Data Transfer Addendum. We sign both. The detail is here.
