Retouch Atelier · 2026-10-08

The correction is also generated.

Most tools that fix an AI-generated product image fix it by generating a new one over the top. For a single hero frame that is usually fine. It stops being fine when the pixels that ship have to be accountable, and when forty images have to match each other.

A good number of tools now exist for this, and the honest position is that for one image, quickly, they are faster than us and cost a fraction of what we do. We would rather say that plainly than pretend the category does not work.

What follows is the part that is harder to find written down: how the fix is actually made, and what that means for the image you put on a product page.

What the tools are genuinely good at

Speed, cost, and no minimum. You upload one frame, brush the wrong area, and get a result in seconds. There is no brief, no onboarding, no invoice to approve. For a single lifestyle image, a social post, or a test of a concept before anyone commits budget, that is the right tool and we will say so to a buyer who asks.

They are also improving quickly. Nothing below is an argument that they do not work.

How the fix is actually made

There are two different operations hiding behind the same word.

The first is regeneration. The tool takes the area you marked, takes a reference you supply, and generates replacement pixels that resemble the reference. This is the usual mechanism, and several vendors describe it openly in their own documentation: the logo is rebuilt from your input. The result can be very close. It is still machine-made.

The second is reconstruction by hand. A retoucher opens the frame alongside the real product and rebuilds the wrong area from the actual artwork — the vector logo, the correct Pantone, the real weave — in layers, by hand. It is slower. The pixels that ship were put there by a person who can be named.

Both are reasonable. They are not interchangeable, and the difference only shows up in three situations.

Where it matters: the reference problem

A tool rebuilds from the reference you hand it. If that reference is low resolution, shot under different light, or quietly the wrong colourway, the tool will still produce something plausible. It has no way to know the reference was wrong, and a plausible result is the dangerous kind, because nobody queries it.

A retoucher looking at a brief with eight colourways in it notices when the reference supplied for one of them does not belong to it. That is not a clever feature. It is just someone paying attention, and it is the thing most often missing from an automated pass.

Where it matters: being able to say how the image was made

You may be asked — by a marketplace, a retail partner, a regulator, or a customer who thinks the product looked different online. The question is how the final image was produced.

If the correction was made by generating new pixels, then the part that shipped was machine-made, whatever the step was called in the interface. That is worth knowing in advance rather than discovering it in an email. We have written separately about where the disclosure line actually sits, which is narrower than most teams assume: does retouched product photography need an AI label?

Where it matters: the set, not the image

This is the one that costs money, and it is invisible until volume.

A tool judges one image at a time. It has no memory of the other seven colourways of that garment and no opinion about whether all eight will sit together in a category grid. Corrected one by one, each result is defensible on its own and the set drifts — a little warmer here, a little more contrast there. The buyer does not notice any single image. They notice the row.

Holding a set together is a different job from fixing a frame. It needs one retoucher across the whole set, a reference that does not move, and someone checking the set as a set before it goes out. The one question to ask — including to us

You do not need to audit anyone. One question separates the two mechanisms:

Were the corrected pixels generated, or reconstructed by hand?

There are three answers you might get, and each tells you something:

“Generated from your reference.” Clear and usable. Now you know, and you can decide whether it matters for that image.

“Reconstructed by hand, and we will put that in writing.” Also clear. Ask for the written version anyway, because an answer you can forward is worth more than one you remember.

A description of the outcome rather than the method — “pixel-perfect”, “studio quality”, “AI-assisted”. That is not an answer to the question. Ask it again.

Common questions

Is a tool or a human studio better for fixing AI product images?

For one image, quickly and cheaply, a tool is often the right choice and we will say so. A hand pass is the right choice in two cases: when the corrected pixels must not themselves be machine-generated, and when a set of images has to stay consistent with each other.

Does a tool that fixes an AI image still count as AI-generated?

If the correction was made by generating new pixels, then the part that shipped was machine-made, whatever the step was called. Whether that triggers any disclosure duty depends on what the image shows rather than on the retouching itself, and we have written about that line separately.

Why can a tool not keep a colourway set consistent?

Because it evaluates one image at a time. Each correction is defensible alone, but without a single reference and one person across the whole set, the set drifts. The inconsistency shows up in a category grid rather than in any single frame.

Will you generate images for us?

No. We correct, retouch and refine images that already exist, including generated ones. We do not produce generated imagery, and that boundary is deliberate rather than a limitation of capacity.

Can you work from a low-resolution product reference?

We can usually work from a poor reference, but we will tell you it is poor rather than quietly inventing the missing detail. If the reference is wrong for the colourway, we will flag it before correcting forty images against it.
