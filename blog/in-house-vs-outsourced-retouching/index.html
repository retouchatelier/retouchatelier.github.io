In-House Retouching vs Outsourcing: What Each Actually Costs Per Image

The two numbers being compared are not the same unit

The comparison is almost always set up like this: a retoucher's salary on one side, a supplier's per-image rate on the other. Then someone divides the salary by a guess at monthly output and the argument starts.

It starts in the wrong place. A salary is a fixed cost — it is paid in a quiet month and a busy one. A per-image rate is a variable cost — it is paid only when frames are sent. Until the fixed side has been converted into a cost per delivered image, there is nothing to compare.

This page is the conversion. It does not tell you what your in-house retouching costs, because that depends on your salaries, your city and your volume. It tells you which lines belong in the number, where the estimate usually breaks, and what the outsourced side costs at published rates.

1. What an in-house image has to carry

A retoucher's salary is the largest line, and it is rarely the only one. The lines below are the ones most often left out of the first estimate.

None of these are arguments against an in-house team. They are simply the lines that have to be in the number before it can be set against a rate card.

2. The formula — and the denominator that breaks it

The arithmetic is straightforward:

Fully loaded monthly cost of the retouching function ÷ images delivered and approved that month = your true cost per image

The numerator is tedious but honest. The denominator is where almost every estimate goes wrong, because people divide by capacity rather than by delivered images.

Capacity assumes every working hour becomes a finished frame. In practice the month also contains briefing, waiting for product or for sign-off, rework, holiday, training, meetings and the days when nothing arrives from the studio. A team that could in theory produce a given number of images will deliver fewer, and the fixed cost is spread over the smaller figure.

This is why the same team can look cheap in one month and expensive in the next without anyone doing anything differently. It is also the single biggest reason an in-house estimate and an outsourced quote disagree: one is measured against capacity, the other against work actually done.

  • Use delivered and approved images, not images started
  • Use a real month, not a good month
  • Count rework once in the cost and once in the time, not once in total
  • If volume is seasonal, run the figure over a year rather than a quarter

4. Where the line falls

There is no universal break-even volume, and anyone who quotes one is guessing at your salaries. What can be said is which way each variable pushes.

Most studios find they are not choosing one. The steady baseline stays in-house and the peak goes out, which is why white-label overflow is the common arrangement rather than wholesale replacement.

5. When keeping it in-house is the right answer

It is worth saying plainly, because a page written by a studio has an obvious incentive not to.

Keep it in-house when the retouching is creative rather than repetitive and needs someone to lean over the screen and change their mind. Keep it in-house when volume is steady enough that the team is busy every day. Keep it in-house when the images genuinely cannot leave, or when the retouching is itself the thing you sell and the skill is the business.

In those cases the fixed cost is buying something a per-image rate does not: presence, iteration and control. Paying more per image for that is not an error.

6. What the cost per image does not tell you

Three things usually decide the outcome, and none of them appear in either number.

  • Turnaround. A cheaper image that arrives after the listing was due has already cost more than its rate. Our live work comes back in 24 to 48 hours with no rush premium, and free test files within six hours
  • Consistency across the batch. The difference buyers notice is not image 1. It is image 4,000, and whether it still matches. This is what the consistency standard exists to measure
  • What happens when volume triples. A team of four cannot absorb a campaign month. Our floor runs 1,800+ images a day and up to 40,000 a month across 58 retouchers on three shifts, six days a week

A supplier whose rate is slightly higher and whose batch arrives whole, on time and matching is usually cheaper once rework and delay are counted.

7. Settle it with your own numbers

An estimate built from someone else's assumptions will not end the argument inside your business. One real batch will.

  • Take a batch you have already produced in-house, and note what it genuinely cost and how long it took end to end
  • Send the same frames out and compare like for like — same brief, same output spec
  • Judge at 100 per cent, not at thumbnail size, and look across the set rather than at one frame
  • Check the fourth day as well as the first: consistency is what fails at volume

FAQ

Common questions

How do I work out the real cost of an in-house retouched image?

Take the fully loaded monthly cost of the retouching function and divide it by the images actually delivered and approved that month. Fully loaded means salary plus employer costs, the share of a supervisor's and a QC checker's time, software licences, hardware and its replacement cycle, desk space, recruitment and training, and cover for absence. The mistake is dividing by capacity rather than by delivered images.

Why is comparing a retoucher's salary to a per-image rate misleading?

They are different units. A salary is a fixed monthly cost that is paid whether or not images arrive. A per-image rate is a variable cost that is only paid when work is sent. Until the salary is converted into a cost per delivered image, the two numbers cannot be set against each other at all.

At what volume does in-house retouching make sense?

There is no universal threshold, because it depends on how steady the volume is rather than how large it is. A team that is busy every working day of the month spreads its fixed cost thinly; the same team at half capacity doubles the cost of every image it does deliver. Steady, predictable volume favours in-house. Volume that arrives in peaks favours a supplier, because the quiet months are not paid for.

When is keeping retouching in-house the better choice?

When the work is creative rather than repetitive and needs an art director in the room, when volume is steady enough to keep the team busy, when the images cannot leave the building for contractual or regulatory reasons, or when the retouching is itself the product you sell. In those cases the fixed cost buys something a per-image rate does not.

What does the cost per image not tell you?

Three things that usually decide the outcome: how quickly a batch comes back, whether image 4,000 still matches image 1, and what happens when volume triples for a month. A cheaper image that arrives late, drifts across a batch or cannot scale for a campaign costs more than its rate suggests.
