Photo Retouching Cost & Rates (2026 Guide)

Photo retouching costs between about $0.50 and $30 per image in 2026. Where a job lands inside that range has almost nothing to do with the studio's postcode and almost everything to do with how many decisions a retoucher has to make on each frame.

Below are the current rates by service, then the part most price guides leave out: what the difference actually looks like, on real images, so you can see what you are paying for.

What you are actually paying for

A price list tells you the number. It does not tell you why one garment costs four times another. These are four real jobs from our own floor, in order of what they cost, with the specific thing that sets the price.

The pattern is consistent. You are not paying for time at a desk. You are paying for how much of the image has to be decided rather than corrected. A cut-out corrects. A ghost mannequin decides.

Per image or per hour?

Retouching is priced two ways.

Per image — used by most studios and outsourcing partners. You know the cost before you place the order, and you pay for finished frames rather than effort. For a catalogue this is almost always both cheaper and easier to budget.

Per hour — common with individual freelancers, roughly $25 to $150+ an hour depending on skill and market. It suits campaign and editorial work where nobody can say in advance how many rounds a single hero image will take.

A useful test: if you can describe the job as a repeatable specification, buy per image. If you cannot, buy per hour.

What moves the price

  • Complexity — a rigid product on white against multi-path jewellery with reflections is not a small difference. It is the difference between one path and forty.
  • Reconstruction — anything the camera never saw has to be built. Inner backs, collar undersides, sleeve openings, hems.
  • Material — sheer, fur, knit, chrome and glass all behave badly at the edge and none of them can be cut with a plain path.
  • Volume — a monthly commitment lets a studio hold a named team on your account instead of rebuilding context every batch. That is the real reason rates fall with volume, and it is why one-off batches cost more per image than the same work on a retainer.
  • Turnaround — rush work carries a premium because it displaces other scheduled work, not because it is done differently.
  • Revisions — unlimited revisions price slightly higher and usually cost less overall, because a re-edit at your end is more expensive than a correction at ours.

The honest floor on volume

Most volume pricing tables stop at "contact us for enterprise rates". Here is the part that is more useful to you.

Below roughly 500 images a month, a high-volume studio is usually the wrong choice — including this one. The per-image rate never reaches its lower tiers, and you are paying for capacity and account management built for catalogue work you do not have. At that size a good freelancer or a small studio will serve you better and probably cheaper.

Above that, the arithmetic reverses quickly, because the fixed cost of understanding your standard gets spread across more frames.

In-house or outsourced?

A full-time retoucher costs roughly $2,000–$5,000+ a month in salary alone, before software, hardware, management time and cover for holiday and sickness. One person also has a hard ceiling on output and no second pair of eyes.

Outsourcing converts that into a per-image or retainer cost that moves with demand. You pay for finished work, you can absorb a campaign spike without hiring, and quality control is somebody else's headcount. The trade is that you have to buy a standard rather than manage one — which is why the test edit matters more than the price list.

What cheap retouching actually costs

The rate is only the visible part of the price. Three costs sit underneath it:

  • Re-editing. Work that comes back wrong is paid for twice — once to the studio, once in your own team's time.
  • Inconsistency. A single image can be judged on its own. A category page cannot. Eight colourways that do not match each other is a problem no individual image reveals.
  • Returns. Colour that is close but not correct is the most expensive error in apparel, because nobody catches it in approval and the customer catches it on arrival.

How to compare studios properly

  • Send your hardest images, not your easiest. Every studio can cut out a shoe. Send the sheer top, the chrome bottle, the black-on-black.
  • Ask what happens on revision two. The answer tells you whether there is a QC layer or a single retoucher hoping.
  • Ask who actually does the work. In-house team, contracted partners, or both — and whether every image passes the same QC either way.
  • Check the paperwork before the price. Will they sign your DPA? What happens to your files afterwards, and on what date?
  • Judge the test edit on your own images. A portfolio shows a studio's best day. Your files show its normal one.

Common questions

What are typical photo retouching rates in 2026?

Between roughly $0.50 and $30 per image. Clipping path and colour correction sit at the bottom, ghost mannequin and e-commerce product work in the middle at $1–$4, jewellery and watches at $2–$8, and high-end beauty or editorial from $10 to $30 and up.

How much does it cost to retouch a product photo?

For standard e-commerce product work, $1–$4 an image is the usual band. Retouch Atelier starts at $1 and the rate falls as monthly volume rises.

How much does a photo retoucher charge per hour?

Freelance retouchers typically charge $25–$150+ an hour depending on skill and market. For repeatable catalogue work, per-image pricing is almost always cheaper and far easier to budget.

Why do two studios quote such different prices for the same image?

Usually because they are not quoting the same work. The cheaper quote often excludes reconstruction — the inner back of a garment, the underside of a collar, a rebuilt reflection — and excludes a separate QC pass. Send both studios the same difficult file and the difference becomes visible immediately.

Is outsourcing photo retouching cheaper than hiring in-house?

For most brands and photographers, yes. A full-time retoucher costs $2,000–$5,000+ a month plus software, management and cover. Outsourcing converts that into a cost that scales with actual demand.

What is the minimum volume worth outsourcing?

Around 500 images a month is the point where high-volume studio pricing starts to make sense. Below that, a freelancer or small studio will usually serve you better.
