Top to bottom that is more than a tenfold spread, for something described on every one of those pages in almost identical words. So what is the extra actually buying?

1. The rate is a time budget, and nothing else

A cut-out is labour. Software is the same price for everyone, the files are the same size, the file transfer costs nothing worth counting. What differs is how many minutes of a person's attention one image can be given.

You do not need anyone's salary figures to see the consequence. $2 is about ten and a half times $0.19. Whatever you believe a competent retoucher's hour is worth, the cheaper rate buys about a tenth of the minutes — and it has to cover the supplier's overhead and margin out of that tenth too.

That is the whole mechanism. Everything below is just where those missing minutes come from.

2. What gets dropped first

As the rate falls, things come out in a fairly predictable order. The service name on the invoice does not change at any point.

DroppedWhat you notice, and when
A second pair of eyesNothing, until the batch that went out had three bad frames in it
Consistency across the batchNot on image 1. On the category page, where the margins and backgrounds do not agree
Difficult edgesHair, lace, mesh and transparency come back cut with scissors, or with a halo
Real contact shadowProducts float. The page looks like a template rather than a catalogue
The hand-drawn path itselfReplaced by an automatic selection: chewed corners, invented edges on pale products

Notice that the first two are invisible on any single image. That is why a test of one frame tells you very little, and why cheap suppliers often test extremely well.

3. Where the cheap rate is the right answer

It genuinely often is, and a studio writing this page has an obvious reason not to say so.

If your product is hard-edged and opaque, sits against a background it separates from cleanly, and the image is judged on a listing at screen size — a low rate is correct, and paying more buys you nothing you can see. Trainers on white. Boxed electronics. Bottles with a defined silhouette. Thousands of them, all shot the same way.

Paying $2 for that image is not buying quality. It is buying minutes the image does not need.

4. Where it costs more than it saves

The rate stops being the relevant number as soon as anything comes back.

  • Rework is paid twice — once to the supplier, and once in your own team's time checking and sending it back
  • A missed listing date costs more than the entire invoice, whatever the invoice says
  • Fixing a batch in-house at your own loaded cost is the most expensive retouching there is
  • An inconsistent category page is a cost you never see on a line item at all

The number worth comparing between suppliers is not cost per image sent. It is cost per accepted image — and on difficult work the cheapest rate is often not the cheapest of those.

5. What this studio charges, and where it sits

Our published rate for clipping path and background removal is $0.50 to $2 an image, with volume discounts published rather than negotiated: 10% from 1,000 images a month, 15% from 3,000, 20% from 5,000, and a custom rate agreed on a call above 10,000.

The band is wide for a reason. A single-path trainer and a multi-path handbag with straps, buckles and holes are both "clipping path", and one takes several times longer than the other. A supplier quoting one flat number for both is either averaging, or not doing one of them properly.

6. How to settle it on your own frames

Price comparison on a rate card will not tell you this. Four frames will.

  • One translucent item — glass, chiffon, a mesh panel
  • One pale product on a pale background, where there is barely an edge to find
  • One with fine edges — hair, fur, lace
  • One where a real contact shadow has to survive the cut-out

Send the same four to every supplier you are considering. Judge at 100 per cent, not at thumbnail size. Then look across all four together rather than at the best one, because consistency across a set is what fails at volume, not any single cut-out.

FAQ

Common questions

Why do clipping path prices range from $0.19 to $2 an image?

Because the rate sets the time budget. The spread from $0.19 to $2 is roughly tenfold, so the attention one image can be given is roughly tenfold different. Nothing else about the service description changes. Work backwards from whatever you believe a retoucher's hour costs and the cheaper rate buys a fraction of the minutes.

Is a cheaper clipping path supplier worse?

Not necessarily, and not on the right work. On a hard-edged opaque product with strong separation from its background, judged as a single image at screen size, a low rate can be entirely correct and paying more buys nothing. The rate becomes a problem when the work needs minutes the rate cannot pay for.

What gets dropped first when the per-image rate falls?

In roughly this order: a second pair of eyes checking the work, consistency across the batch, treatment of difficult edges such as hair, lace and transparency, genuine contact shadows, and finally the hand-drawn path itself, replaced by an automatic selection. The service name on the invoice stays the same throughout.

Does a low rate per image mean a low total cost?

Only if nothing comes back. Rework is paid twice, once to the supplier and once in your own team's time, and a batch that misses a listing date costs more than the whole invoice. The number worth comparing is cost per accepted image, not cost per image sent.

How can I compare suppliers at different prices fairly?

Send every supplier the same four difficult frames: one translucent item, one pale product on a pale background, one with fine edges such as hair or lace, and one where a real contact shadow has to survive. Judge at 100 per cent rather than at thumbnail size, and look across all four rather than at the best one. Consistency across the set is what fails at volume.
